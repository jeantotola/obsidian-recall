# Quiz rules

Recall reads this file before every /quiz. Edit it to change how questions are asked and judged.

## Asking

- One question per concept, and atomic: it tests one idea and has one answer. Don't chain two questions with "and".
- The answer is direct: no calculation is needed to reach it.
- It must make sense years from now: no author, book or course trivia, and definitions that still hold outside the current topic.
- Ask from the concept's owner page, not from general knowledge. When the page can't support a good question, ask what it allows and flag the page.
- Test the concept's core idea: what it is, what it's for, how it differs from its neighbours. To differ from the recent questions, vary the wording or apply the idea to a case; don't move on to peripheral facts of the page (examples, syntax, lists of parts), which are there to explain, not to be tested. When the concept has a focus, ask about the new content. When it has a gap, ask about the idea the gap names.
- Free recall: never multiple choice, and never a yes/no question that gives the answer away.
- The goal is to consolidate memory, not to catch the user out.

## Judging

- Pass when the core idea is present and there is no conceptual error; the wording doesn't matter.
- Terse answers (keywords, fragments, arrows) are fine: judge the idea, not the prose. When a terse answer is ambiguous between knowing and guessing, ask one short follow-up instead of failing.
- A mistake that comes only from a prerequisite with its own concept doesn't fail the concept being asked; /quiz offers to open a gap on the prerequisite instead.
- Be strict: an answer that misses the core idea or contains a conceptual error is a fail. Lenient grading stretches intervals for nothing.
- On a pass, say only "pass", unless the answer has a flaw that doesn't fail it: a list left incomplete, an imprecise technical term, or a problem in the logic. Then add one line that names the flaw and gives the precise version. No expected answer, no page.
- On a fail, say fail, give one line of justification, the expected answer in one or two lines, and the page.
- The user may contest a verdict. Their word wins.
